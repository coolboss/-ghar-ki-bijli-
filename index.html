Stage 1 of 7 · 66 kV → 11 kV

MGVCL substation

Gujarat splits electricity into three businesses: GSECL and private plants generate, GETCO transmits at high voltage, and MGVCL distributes it to Vadodara and nine other districts. Your MGVCL substation steps 66 kV down to 11 kV and sends it out on feeders.

Open for detail
Generation and transmission (GETCO)

Thermal, gas, solar and wind plants produce at 11 to 21 kV. A generator transformer steps this up to 220 or 400 kV. Higher voltage means lower current for the same power, and losses in a wire grow with the square of current, so long lines run at the highest practical voltage. GETCO towers carry it across the state.

Grid substation, 220/66 kV

Large outdoor yards with the big grey transformers you see near the highway. GETCO hands over to MGVCL at the 66 kV level.

MGVCL substation, 66/11 kV (your "sub-station")

This is the one near Gotri, Harni or Akota that your bill refers to. Two or three power transformers step 66 kV down to 11 kV. From here several 11 kV feeders leave, each a named cable or overhead line serving a few thousand consumers. A feeder breaker trips here when a tree falls on a line, which is why a whole area loses power at once.

MGVCL complaint numbers quote your feeder name and DT (distribution transformer) number. Both are worth writing down for your house.

Stage 2 of 7 · 11 kV → 433 V

Distribution transformer on the pole

The drum on the two-pole structure in your lane. 11 kV goes in; three phases and a neutral come out at 433 V. The neutral is earthed here, which is why it sits near 0 V.

Open for detail
Distribution transformer (DT) on the pole or plinth

The green or grey drum on a two-pole structure near your lane, typically 63, 100, 200 or 315 kVA. Its primary takes 11 kV from the feeder; its secondary gives three phases + neutral at 433 V (nominally 415 V after a little drop). The neutral is earthed here with a buried electrode; that earth is what makes your neutral sit at 0 V.

A DT serves 50 to 300 homes. Overloaded DTs are the reason voltage dips to 200 V on summer evenings when every AC in the lane starts.

Stage 3 of 7 · 415 V · 3Ø + N

LT line along the street

Four conductors run past your gate: R, Y, B and neutral. Any phase to neutral is 230 V; phase to phase is 415 V. Your house taps one phase, or all three.

Open for detail
LT line (low tension) along the street

Four bare or insulated conductors on the poles outside your gate: R, Y, B and neutral. In newer colonies this is an aerial bunched cable (black twisted bundle) or an underground armoured cable with a green feeder pillar box on the footpath. Street lights hang off one phase of this line.

Single phase vs three phase

The generator at the power station makes three alternating voltages at once, each shifted by one-third of a cycle. These are the R, Y and B phases. Every home gets one of them, or all three.

Single phase · 230 V

  • Two wires from the pole: one phase (red) and neutral (black), plus your own earth.
  • Usually sanctioned up to about 5 kW by MGVCL. Enough for lights, fans, fridge, TV, one or two ACs and a geyser if not all run together.
  • Single-phase meter, double-pole main MCB.
  • The voltage between phase and neutral is 230 V.

Three phase · 415 V

  • Four wires from the pole: R, Y, B phases and neutral. Each phase to neutral is still 230 V; phase to phase is 415 V (230 × √3).
  • Needed when sanctioned load is larger (bungalow with 3 to 4 ACs, lift, submersible pump, a clinic). Three-phase meter and a four-pole main switch.
  • The electrician balances the house: ground-floor rooms on R, first floor on Y, kitchen and ACs on B, so each phase carries a similar load.
  • If one phase fails at the substation, only one-third of your house goes dark. That is the usual reason "half the colony has power".
Why the neutral matters in three phase. When the three phases are balanced, their currents cancel and the neutral carries almost nothing. If the neutral wire breaks (a loose joint at the pole or a corroded lug in your meter box), the phases are no longer anchored to 0 V: a lightly loaded phase can rise toward 415 V and burn every appliance on it. Electricians call this a neutral float. It is the single most common cause of "all my electronics died at once".
Stage 4 of 7 · 230 V · 1Ø + N

Service cable and meter

The service cable brings one phase and the neutral to your meter. Everything up to and including the meter belongs to MGVCL. Everything after it is yours.

Open for detail
Service connection to your house

MGVCL's lineman taps one phase (or all three) and the neutral and runs a service cable to your building: usually a 2-core or 4-core aluminium or copper cable, 10 to 25 mm². Everything up to and including the meter belongs to MGVCL; everything after the meter is yours.

Energy meter (increasingly a smart prepaid meter)

Counts kWh. A single-phase meter has 4 terminals: phase in, phase out, neutral in, neutral out. The three-phase meter has 8. It is sealed; opening the seal is an offence. MGVCL's smart meters report readings over a radio network and can be prepaid.

Right after the meter MGVCL usually fits a cut-out or a small MCB sized to your sanctioned load. Pull more than the sanction and it trips, which is your prompt to apply for load enhancement.

Smart meters: when the bill goes up

MGVCL is replacing old meters with prepaid smart meters. The first bill afterwards is often higher, and the meter gets blamed. Separate two questions before you complain: did the units change, or only the rupees?

Case A · units similar, rupees higher

Old meter 420 kWh, new meter 425 kWh, bill much higher. The meter is not the story. Look at the fixed charge, a tariff slab change, time-of-day rates, arrears carried from estimated bills, or a security deposit adjustment. Read the bill line by line.

Case B · units jumped, usage unchanged

Old meter 420 kWh, new meter 650 kWh, same appliances. Now metrology, wiring or a neighbour's load on your meter deserve a real test. Old electromechanical meters often under-recorded for years, so the new one may be the honest one, but check.

Known-load test · do it yourself in 30 minutes
  1. Write down the meter reading (kWh, to three decimals on a smart meter) and the time.
  2. Switch off every circuit MCB. The reading should stop moving. If it still climbs, something is wired before your board, or the meter is wrong.
  3. Switch on one known resistive load: a 2 kW geyser or a 1.5 kW room heater. Run it exactly 30 minutes. Expected: 1.0 kWh for 2 kW.
  4. Compare. Meters are allowed about ± 1 to 2 % error (Class 1). A 10 % difference is a complaint; a 2 % difference is not.

Stage 5 of 7 · MAIN · RCCB · MCB

Distribution board

Current passes the main switch, then the RCCB, then one MCB for each circuit. The MCB protects the wire from fire. The RCCB protects people from shock.

Open for detail
Your side begins: main isolator and distribution board

From the meter, your own cable (6 or 10 mm² copper for single phase) runs to the distribution board (DB) inside the house. The chapters below open the board up.

MCB, RCCB, ELCB, RCBO, MCCB: who protects whom

Each device watches for one kind of trouble. A house with only MCBs is protected against fire but not against electrocution. Learn the three faults first, then the devices.

Fault 1

Overload

Too many appliances on one wire. The current creeps above what the wire can carry. The wire warms, the insulation softens, and over minutes it can start a fire inside the wall.

Fault 2

Short circuit

Live touches neutral directly: a nail through a cable, a burnt plug. The current jumps to hundreds or thousands of amperes in milliseconds. A flash, a bang, melted copper.

Fault 3

Earth leakage

Current escapes to earth through something it should not: the body of a geyser, wet fingers, a child's hand. Only 30 mA (0.03 A) across the heart can be fatal. An MCB rated 16 A will never notice it.

Tap a device

The order of current is: meter → main DP MCB → RCCB → live busbar → individual MCB → red wire to the room → appliance → black wire back → neutral bar → meter → MGVCL. The green wire never carries current unless something is wrong.

Why this order?

  1. Main MCB first so the whole board can be killed with one lever, and so the RCCB itself is protected against short circuit.
  2. RCCB second, watching everything downstream. One leak anywhere trips the house. (RCBOs per circuit avoid that.)
  3. Circuit MCBs last, one per group of outlets, so a fault in the kitchen does not darken the bedrooms.

Sizing rules an electrician uses

  • Wire carrying capacity: 1.5 mm² ≈ 16 A, 2.5 mm² ≈ 22 A, 4 mm² ≈ 30 A, 6 mm² ≈ 38 A (copper, in conduit). The MCB is always rated below the wire.
  • Max 800 W per lighting circuit (about 10 points); max 3 kW per socket circuit (about 2 to 3 16 A sockets).
  • Every AC, geyser, oven and washing machine gets its own circuit on a DP MCB.
  • Leave 20 % spare ways in the DB; the next renovation will thank you.
MDB and SDB: dividing a bigger house

A flat has one board. A bungalow, a two-floor house or a clinic has a Main Distribution Board (MDB) near the meter and a Sub-Distribution Board (SDB) per floor or zone. The tree gets one more level, and in a three-phase home each SDB's circuits are spread across R, Y and B so no phase carries the whole house.

The hierarchy

Rules at each level

  • MDB: 4-pole isolator or MCCB after the three-phase meter, SPD, one 4-pole MCB per outgoing SDB feeder. No final circuits here except the pump and lift.
  • SDB: a zone board. Its outgoing single-phase circuits take live from R, Y or B busbars in turn. Each circuit keeps its own neutral back to the SDB neutral bar.
  • Never share a neutral between circuits on different RCBOs or different phases. Two phases sharing a neutral can put 415 V across an appliance if that neutral breaks, and RCBOs trip at random.
  • Label every board: MDB-01, SDB-GF, SDB-FF, SDB-UPS. The Passport uses these names.
Phase balancing tool · assign each circuit to a phase

An unbalanced house is the hidden reason "the lights dim when the AC starts". Aim for all three phases within about 20 % of each other.

Inverter and UPS: a second, smaller tree

An inverter does not "add power to the house". It feeds its own separate board, the Essential SDB, and only the circuits wired from that board stay on when MGVCL goes off. Choosing those circuits is the whole design.

  • The inverter's input is a circuit from the normal board (16 A MCB). Its output goes only to SDB-UPS, which has its own RCCB or RCBOs.
  • UPS sockets get red face plates so nobody plugs an iron or geyser into the battery.
  • A UPS-fed phase returns on a UPS neutral, back to SDB-UPS. Never borrow a normal-circuit neutral for a UPS point; the RCCBs on both sides will trip, and the inverter can back-feed a "dead" wire.
  • In a three-phase home the single-phase inverter sits on one phase (its input) and feeds SDB-UPS alone. It does not merge R, Y and B.
  • Earth stays common to the whole building. Whether the inverter bonds neutral to earth in backup mode depends on its design; follow the manual, never add a link yourself.

Wires inside the wall

From the MCB a red wire carries live to the room, a black wire brings the current back, and a green wire waits for a fault. The wire is sized so the MCB trips before the wire heats.

The classic trap. In the wall, black = neutral. In a three-phase appliance cord, black = a live phase. And in the wall, blue = B phase (live), while in a cord light blue = neutral. Never assume by colour alone across the two systems. A tester or multimeter is the final word.

Other colours you will meet

  • White or grey in old 1980s wiring: often neutral, sometimes a switched live. Test it.
  • Red with a tape marker: a switched live coming back from a switch to the fan or light (electricians call it the "return" or "switch wire").
  • Two reds in a 2-way staircase switch: the "strappers" between the two switches.
  • Yellow sleeve on a red: some electricians mark the Y phase this way when only red wire is on the roll. Discourage it.

Buying wire · what the print on the cable means

  • 1.5 sq mm FR: cross-section 1.5 mm², flame retardant. Lights and fans.
  • 2.5 sq mm FRLS: flame retardant, low smoke. 6 A and 16 A sockets.
  • 4 / 6 sq mm: AC, geyser, kitchen 16 A points; 6 mm² for the main.
  • 1100 V grade: insulation rated for 1,100 V; standard for house wiring.
  • ISI mark + IS 694: the BIS standard. Insist on it. Copper only; aluminium is false economy indoors.
The quiet faults that actually burn houses

Short circuits are loud and get caught by the MCB in milliseconds. The faults that start fires and kill appliances are slow, hidden, and look fine on the day the house is handed over.

heat

Loose terminal

A screw not tightened, or aluminium wire that has crept. The joint gains a few hundredths of an ohm of resistance. Power lost there is I²R: current squared times resistance. At 20 A, 0.05 Ω wastes 20 W inside a plastic socket, all day. The plastic browns, then chars. No MCB sees it, because the current is normal.

415 V

Broken neutral

On three-phase, a corroded neutral lug at the pole or the meter lets each phase's voltage wander. The lightly loaded floor sees 300 to 400 V and every TV, inverter and fridge board dies at once. The only defence is a tight, tested neutral and an over/under-voltage relay in the MDB.

trips

Mixed neutrals

Two circuits on two RCBOs sharing one neutral. Each RCBO sees current leaving on its live that returns on the other's neutral: an imbalance. Random trips, usually blamed on "a faulty RCCB". The fix is one neutral per circuit, back to the board.

dark

One phase lost

A feeder fuse at the DT blows for Y phase. Bedrooms on Y go dark, the hall on R works. Residents report "half the house has no light". Before calling MGVCL, check the three phase indicator lamps on the MDB; if only one is dark, it is the supply.

hidden

Joints inside conduit or plaster

A wire that was 30 cm short, twisted and taped inside the pipe. Every such joint is a loose terminal with no screw at all. Joints belong only in junction boxes you can open. Photographs before plaster are the only way to know where they are.

guess

Unlabelled board

The electrician must switch off "something" to work on a socket. He guesses, works on a live wire, or kills the fridge and the Wi-Fi for an hour. A documentation fault that behaves like a wiring fault. The Passport fixes it.

Stage 7 of 7 · SOCKET · SWITCH

The point in the room

At the point, the switch always breaks the red wire. In a 3-pin socket, earth is on top, live on the right and neutral on the left.

Open for detail
Wiring a room, point by point

Pick a room to see its circuits, then look at how a switch, a fan and a socket are actually connected.

The one rule of switches

A switch always breaks the red wire

Live (red) comes from the DB to the switch. From the switch a second red (the "return") goes to the bulb. Neutral (black) goes straight from the DB to the bulb, never through the switch. When the switch is off, the bulb holder has no pressure on it. If an electrician puts the switch on the black wire, the bulb goes off but the holder stays live, and you get a shock changing a bulb.

Look at a 3-pin socket from the front

Earth on top, live on the right

Indian 6 A and 16 A sockets (type D and M plugs): the large pin at the top is earth, the bottom-right hole is live, bottom-left is neutral. The earth pin is longer so it connects first and breaks last. A 16 A socket must be on 2.5 mm² wire minimum.

Ceiling fan with regulator

Red from the switch goes to the regulator, regulator output goes to the fan's live, fan's other wire (usually black or blue from the factory) goes to neutral. The capacitor inside the fan is on the fan's own two wires; never cut it out. A fan point is 1.5 mm² on the 10 A fan MCB.

Two-way switch (staircase, bedroom door + bedside)

Both switches are special 2-way switches with three terminals. Live goes to the common of switch 1, the two reds ("strappers") join the L1/L2 of both switches, and the common of switch 2 goes to the light. Either switch toggles the light. A common mistake is using ordinary 1-way switches; it looks the same but one switch then becomes a master.

Geyser and AC points

Each on its own circuit, on a double-pole MCB so both live and neutral are cut, with a 16 A or 20 A socket or a direct DP switch at the point. 4 mm² for anything above 2 kW or longer than 20 m. The earth wire must reach the geyser body; a geyser without earth plus a weak RCCB is the classic bathroom fatality.

Looping and junction boxes

Neutral and earth are "looped": one wire runs point to point, joined only inside switchboards and metal junction boxes, never inside the conduit. A joint hidden in a wall is a fault waiting for a monsoon. Every conduit has a fish wire left in for future pulling.

Earthing: the wire that saves lives

The green wire does nothing on a normal day. On the day a geyser element cracks and its metal body goes live, it carries the fault into the ground so hard that the RCCB and MCB trip before anyone touches the tap.

How an earth pit is built (IS 3043)

  1. A pit about 2 to 3 m deep near the house, in moist soil, away from the septic tank.
  2. An electrode: a 2.5 m galvanised pipe or a 600 × 600 mm copper plate, or a modern chemical earthing rod with a bentonite / conductive backfill.
  3. Layers of charcoal and salt (old method) or the chemical compound to keep the soil conductive.
  4. A 6 or 8 mm² green copper wire (or 25 × 3 mm GI strip) from the electrode to the DB's earth bar, with a test link in a small chamber on top.
  5. Measured with an earth tester. Target: below 1 Ω for a hospital or clinic; below 5 Ω is the practical minimum for a home.

Mistakes that make earthing useless

  • Earth wire connected to the neutral bar "because neutral is also earthed". This defeats the RCCB.
  • Earth wire tied to a water pipe. Plastic pipes have replaced metal; there is no path.
  • Pit dried out in summer; nobody has watered it in years. Chemical earthing exists to avoid this.
  • Top earth pin on the socket left unconnected to save wire. Test every socket with a plug tester.
  • Two separate earths for the inverter and the house at different potentials.
The three-second test every owner can do. Buy a ₹300 socket tester. Plug it into every 3-pin socket. Three lamps light in the "correct" pattern only if live, neutral and earth are all on the right pins. Then press the TEST button on the RCCB once a month; it must trip instantly. If it does not, the RCCB is dead and your house is protected only against fire, not against shock.
New to this? Four words you need: volt, ampere, watt, unit

Think of electricity as water in a pipe. That one picture carries you through everything else.

Volt (V) · pressure

How hard the push is

Voltage is the pressure in the pipe. Your home is supplied at a nominal 230 V (older books say 240 V). The 11,000 V on the street pole is the same water at 48 times the pressure. Pressure is what gives you a shock; it does not need to flow much to hurt you.

Ampere (A) · flow

How much is flowing

Current is litres per second. A 9 W LED bulb draws about 0.04 A; a 1.5-ton air-conditioner draws 6 to 8 A; a 2 kW geyser draws nearly 9 A. Wires, switches and MCBs are all rated in amperes because flow is what heats a wire.

Watt (W) · power

Pressure × flow

Power = Volt × Ampere. 230 V × 8.7 A ≈ 2,000 W = 2 kW. This is the number printed on every appliance. 1,000 W is 1 kW. Your sanctioned load on the MGVCL bill is in kW.

Unit (kWh) · energy

Power × time, what you pay for

One unit = 1 kW running for 1 hour. The 2 kW geyser on for 30 minutes = 1 unit. The meter counts units; MGVCL bills you per unit in slabs.

Wire size and MCB are chosen so the wire can carry the current without heating, and the MCB trips before the wire does.

AC, not DC

Mains electricity alternates direction 50 times a second (50 Hz). That is why a transformer can step it up and down, and why "live" and "neutral" are not plus and minus.

Live / Phase

The wire that carries the pressure. Touch it while standing on the ground and current flows through you. Every switch must break this wire.

Neutral

The return path. It is tied to earth at the transformer, so it sits near 0 V. It still carries the full current of the circuit; never treat it as safe.

Earth

A third wire that normally carries nothing. It connects the metal body of your fridge or geyser to the ground, so a fault goes into the earth instead of through you.

The Electrical Passport: a QR code on every meter box

A house is wired once and then opened by a dozen different electricians over forty years. Nobody remembers which MCB feeds the guest-room AC, where the earth pit is, or that the kitchen was moved to the B phase in 2019. The knowledge lives in one man's head, and he moves away.

Faults at 11 pm

The RCCB trips. Which circuit is leaking? With a circuit map you switch MCBs off one by one in two minutes. Without it you call someone in the morning.

Renovation

The contractor wants to drill a wall. The schema shows the conduit routes and the depth. Nails through cables stop being a lottery.

Emergencies

Fire brigade, a tenant, a neighbour: anyone with a phone can find the main switch and the gas line isolation in seconds.

Resale and insurance

A dated, tested wiring record with earth resistance readings raises buyer confidence and answers the insurer's first question after a fire.

Medical and clinics

For a hospital, the map marks which sockets are on the UPS / inverter circuit, so an OT or NICU equipment is never plugged into a raw line.

Cost: a sticker

A laminated QR on the DB door and another on the meter box. The schema itself is a PDF or a web page; the QR is just the pointer. Update the page, keep the sticker.

What belongs in the passport

  • Single-line diagram: meter → MDB → SDBs → final circuits, with phase of each.
  • DB schedule: every MCB/RCBO number, rating, curve, wire size, what it serves.
  • Floor map with a point ID on every switch, socket, fan and AC.
  • Conduit routes and photos taken before plaster and false ceiling.
  • Earth pit location, electrode type, resistance reading and date.
  • UPS map: which board, which sockets, inverter rating.
  • Test sheet at commissioning: insulation (MΩ), polarity, RCCB trip time, earth continuity.
  • Change log: every later modification, by whom, when.

Core idea: the wiring belongs to the building, so the record stays with the building, not with the electrician.

Start small: a 20-home pilot
The question worth testing is whether a competent electrician can document an ordinary home with only 30 to 60 extra minutes of work, if the phone workflow is taps, room icons, voice notes and photos. Output per home: single-line diagram, DB schedule, point IDs, phase allocation, cable sizes, UPS map, earth map, test sheet, pre-plaster photos, a printable passport and a QR.

    
Who pays for it
Three customers: the owner ("tell me what is inside my walls"), the electrician ("help me work professionally and charge for it"), and the builder ("a premium handover at little extra cost"). Beyond new homes: mapping existing undocumented houses, an electrical audit service (earthing, polarity, RCCB, phase balance), and electrician certification in diagram reading and RCBO/SPD/UPS practice.

    Professional electrician's checklist for a new home
    
    
      
  • Load list per room, phase balancing plan, and the DB schedule on paper before the first conduit is laid.
    • 
      
  • Copper FR/FRLS, ISI-marked, 1100 V grade; 1.5 / 2.5 / 4 / 6 mm² as per the sizing rules in the Board stage; PVC conduit 20 or 25 mm with fish wire.
    • 
      
  • Red live, black neutral, green earth; separate neutral per circuit, no shared neutrals.
    • 
      
  • Main DP MCB → RCCB 30 mA (Type A if inverter appliances) → circuit MCBs; SPD Type 2; DP MCBs for every wet-area and heavy appliance.
    • 
      
  • Earth pit per IS 3043, measured and recorded; earth continuity at every socket checked with a tester.
    • 
      
  • Insulation resistance test (megger, 500 V) of every circuit: above 1 MΩ. Polarity test of every socket. RCCB trip-time test.
    • 
      
  • Each MCB labelled; schema and test readings printed and encoded in the QR on the DB door.
